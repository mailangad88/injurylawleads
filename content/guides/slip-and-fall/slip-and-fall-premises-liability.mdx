Slip and fall cases are part of an area of law called premises liability. Property owners and managers have a duty to keep their property reasonably safe for visitors. When they fail to, and someone is hurt, they may be responsible for the injuries.

## What you generally need to show

1. **A dangerous condition existed**, such as a wet floor, broken stairs, poor lighting, ice, uneven pavement or loose carpet.
2. **The owner knew or should have known about it.** For example, an employee saw the spill, or it was there long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it.
3. **They failed to fix it or warn you**, for example with a wet-floor sign.
4. **The condition caused your fall and your injuries.**

Proving the owner knew or should have known is often the hardest part, which is why evidence gathered right away matters so much.

## Common places falls happen

- Grocery stores and big-box retailers
- Restaurants and bars
- Apartment complexes and rental properties
- Parking lots and sidewalks
- Hotels, gyms and office buildings

Falls on government property, like a city sidewalk or public building, often have special notice deadlines that can be as short as a few months.

## What to do after a fall

- **Report it** to a manager or property owner and ask for a copy of the incident report.
- **Photograph the hazard** before it is cleaned up, along with your shoes and the surrounding area.
- **Get witness names.**
- **Ask about cameras.** Surveillance footage is often deleted on a schedule. A lawyer can request that it be preserved.
- **See a doctor** promptly, even if you think you just have bruises. Falls frequently cause wrist fractures, hip injuries, back injuries and head trauma.
- **Keep your shoes and clothing** as they were.

## How fault is shared

Owners often argue the injured person should have been watching where they were going. Most states use some form of comparative fault, which reduces compensation by your percentage of responsibility. A handful of states still use contributory negligence, where any fault on your part can bar recovery. An attorney licensed in your state can explain how these rules apply.
