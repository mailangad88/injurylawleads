What you do in the first hours and days after a car accident affects your recovery and any claim you make later. Use this checklist at the scene and in the week that follows.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Get to safety and call 911 if anyone is hurt.
- Take photos and collect names, phone numbers and insurance details.
- See a doctor even if you feel okay. Some injuries appear later.
- Be careful what you say to insurance adjusters.
- Talk to an injury lawyer before you accept a settlement.

</KeyTakeaways>

## At the scene

**1. Check for injuries and call 911.** Your safety comes first. If anyone is hurt, call 911 right away. If the cars can be moved and it is safe, move them out of traffic and turn on your hazard lights.

**2. Call the police.** A police report records who was involved, their insurance information, witness names, and often the officer's observations about what happened. Ask for the report number before you leave.

**3. Exchange information.** Get the other driver's name, phone number, address, driver's license number, license plate, and insurance company and policy number. Take a photo of their license and insurance card if they agree.

**4. Document everything.** Use your phone to photograph the vehicles from several angles, the road, skid marks, traffic signs, weather conditions and any visible injuries. A short video walking around the scene is also helpful.

**5. Talk to witnesses.** Independent witnesses can be the deciding factor when drivers disagree about fault. Get their names and phone numbers.

**6. Don't apologize or guess about fault.** Saying "I'm sorry" is a natural reaction, but it can be treated as an admission. Stick to facts when you talk to the other driver and the police.

## In the days after the crash

**7. Get medical care.** Adrenaline can mask pain. Whiplash, back injuries, and concussions commonly show up a day or more after a crash. See a doctor as soon as possible and follow the treatment plan. Gaps in treatment are one of the most common reasons insurers give for offering less.

**8. Notify your own insurance company.** Most policies require you to report an accident promptly. Give them the basic facts. You do not need to speculate about your injuries or fault.

**9. Be careful with the other driver's insurer.** Their adjuster's job is to limit what their company pays. They may call quickly, sound friendly, and ask for a recorded statement or for you to sign a medical records release. You can politely decline until you have had a chance to get advice.

**10. Keep a file.** Save medical bills, receipts, repair estimates, rental car costs, and records of missed work. Keep a simple journal of your pain, limitations and appointments. These details help show what the accident has cost you.

**11. Stay off social media.** Insurers look at public posts. A photo of you at a family event can be used to argue your injuries are not serious, even if you were in pain the whole time.

## When to talk to a lawyer

You do not need a lawyer for every fender bender. But it is worth talking to one if:

- You or a passenger were injured, especially if you needed an ER visit, ongoing treatment, or time off work.
- Fault is disputed, or the other driver's insurer is blaming you.
- The other driver was uninsured or underinsured.
- A commercial vehicle, rideshare driver, or government vehicle was involved.
- The insurance company has offered a quick settlement.

Most personal injury lawyers offer a free consultation and work on a [contingency fee](/guides/claims-process/how-contingency-fees-work), so you pay nothing up front. Keep in mind that every state has a [deadline for filing an injury lawsuit](/guides/claims-process/statute-of-limitations-personal-injury), and some claims against government agencies have much shorter notice requirements.
