The value of a car accident claim depends on your injuries, who was at fault, how much insurance is available and your state's laws. Anyone who quotes a number before reviewing your records is guessing. This guide explains what goes into a claim's value so you know what to document and what to expect.

## What you may be able to recover

In a typical car accident claim, compensation (called "damages") falls into two groups.

**Economic damages** are the costs you can add up with receipts:

- Medical bills, past and expected future treatment
- Lost wages and lost earning capacity if you cannot return to the same work
- Property damage, such as vehicle repair or replacement
- Out-of-pocket costs like prescriptions, travel to appointments and household help

**Non-economic damages** compensate for losses that do not come with a bill:

- Pain and suffering
- Emotional distress, anxiety and sleep problems
- Loss of enjoyment of life, such as no longer being able to play sports or pick up your kids
- Scarring and disfigurement

In rare cases involving extreme conduct, such as drunk driving, some states allow punitive damages meant to punish the wrongdoer.

## What makes a claim worth more or less

**Severity and permanence of the injury.** A fracture that needs surgery, or an injury that causes lasting limitations, is valued very differently from soft tissue soreness that resolves in a few weeks.

**Quality of medical documentation.** Consistent treatment, clear diagnoses, and doctors who connect your injuries to the crash all strengthen a claim. Gaps in treatment weaken it.

**Clarity of fault.** If the other driver rear-ended you or was cited, liability is easier to prove. If fault is shared, many states reduce your recovery by your percentage of fault, and a few bar recovery entirely if you were even slightly at fault.

**Available insurance.** Recovery is often limited by the at-fault driver's policy limits and your own uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage. An experienced lawyer will look for every source of coverage.

**Your state's rules.** Some states use a no-fault system that requires you to go through your own personal injury protection coverage first and limits when you can sue the other driver. Some states cap certain damages.

## Why early offers tend to be low

Insurance companies often make a quick offer, sometimes before you know the full extent of your injuries. Once you accept and sign a release, you usually cannot reopen the claim, even if you later need surgery. Before accepting, make sure you understand your diagnosis and expected future care.

## Do people with lawyers get more?

It depends on the case. A lawyer can find coverage you didn't know about, document future medical costs and file a lawsuit if the insurer won't pay fairly. Their fee comes out of the recovery, so for a small claim with clear fault the difference may be modest. A free consultation is the quickest way to find out whether a lawyer would add value in your situation.
